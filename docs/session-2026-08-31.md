# Session report — 31 August 2026: closing the design's own criteria

The previous two sessions both ended by asking what the *scorer* should do next. This one
asked what the *project* still owed, checked the answer against `docs/design.md` rather
than against the report's own recommendations, and found three success criteria open —
two of them invisible from anything in `reports/`.

Nothing here changes a metric. Every number that was published on the 28th is still the
number, and notebook 05 re-executed with all pre-existing outputs byte-identical. What
changed is the gap between what the design promised and what the repository contains.

## What was actually missing

The handoff file left on the 28th opened with "the tree is dirty, commit and push" — which
was already false, since all four commits had landed and `main` was in sync. That is the
hazard of a handoff written before the commit: it describes an intention, and the next
session reads it as a state. It has been deleted rather than corrected, and its surviving
content (four research follow-ups) already lives in §9 of the evaluation report and in the
28th's session report, where it is not going to rot.

Checking the design instead of the handoff turned up:

1. **`error_analysis.md` did not exist.** A named deliverable in the success criteria. A
   grep found exactly one reference to the filename in the whole repository — the line in
   `design.md` asking for it.
2. **Intra-scorer consistency had never been measured.** It appears in Premise 4, in the
   Evaluation design, and in the success criteria, and nowhere in `reports/`. It is not
   decoration either: it is the metric that *replaced* inter-rater reliability when gate 2
   established that the corpus ships single consensus labels, so it carries the whole
   reliability half of the deliverable.
3. **Labelled feedback examples had never been built.** `grep -rn feedback` hits
   `design.md` and nothing else. No arm in this project emits prose.

The pattern in all three is the same and worth naming: **the report's own "next steps" list
cannot find work the report never knew it was missing.** §9 has been carefully maintained
across three sessions and lists five recommendations, none of which is any of the above,
because all five were generated by looking at the results. The design document is the only
artefact that remembers the original promises, and nothing had been reading it.

## The one analysis that was worth running

Consolidating existing material into `error_analysis.md` would have been clerical. One
question came up while doing it that had not been asked, and it turned out to be the
substantive part of the session.

§6 of the evaluation report tests whether **overall WER** propagates into scoring error and
finds it does not: raw r = +0.151 collapses to a partial of −0.014 once proficiency is
controlled. That is a test of an aggregate. It is not a test of whether a particular *kind*
of ASR error propagates, and the kinds are not interchangeable here — `n_words` is the
Random Forest's dominant feature, so a transcript that loses words loses the feature the
scorer leans on hardest, while a transcript that swaps one word for another leaves it
untouched. Averaging the two into a single WER figure is exactly the operation that would
hide one inside the other.

Splitting WER into substitution, deletion and insertion rates over a common denominator:

| Variable | Raw r vs signed error | Partial (score held) |
|---|---|---|
| WER (lenient) | +0.151 (p = .0002) | −0.014 (p = .733) |
| substitution rate | +0.242 (p < .0001) | −0.101 (p = .013) |
| deletion rate | +0.082 (p = .044) | **−0.119 (p = .004)** |
| insertion rate | +0.107 (p = .009) | +0.017 (p = .683) |

Two things fell out. The first is that a residual survives where the aggregate showed
none — the WER test was averaging an effect against nothing and correctly reporting
nothing. The second is more useful: **the surviving effect has the opposite sign to the
alarm**. The raw correlation in step 1 warns about over-scoring badly-transcribed
responses. The residual, once proficiency is held constant, is *negative* — a transcript
with words missing is **under**-scored. Mechanically that is what a word-count-dominated
feature set predicts, which is the reason the split was worth doing rather than a surprise.

It replicates on the LoRA arm — a scorer that reads nothing but the words, so a different
mechanism entirely — at −0.114 (p = .005). Same sign, near-identical magnitude. The rows
are the same 600, so that is an independent *scorer* over shared data, which rules out a
feature-pipeline artefact and not a property of the responses.

**How hard to push this: not hard.** Both surviving correlations sit near r = −0.11, about
1% of the variance in signed error, against a band-compression term of nearly a full band.
Five variables were tested, so one false positive is expected at α = .05; deletion rate
survives a Bonferroni correction across the five and substitution rate does not. The
evaluation report's operational conclusion is unchanged and its phrasing is now weaker: the
null holds for aggregate WER, and not quite for deletion.

## The case classes, and what the licence does to them

The criterion asks for five concrete ASR-error→score-error cases. The conventional form of
that — five bad transcripts quoted beside their scores — is precisely what the licence
forbids committing. The report delivers five **classes** instead, each defined by a
measurable property of the ASR output, cut at the sample's own deciles rather than round
thresholds, and carrying its count, its mean WER, its mean human score and the scorer's
behaviour.

Two of the five are informative in ways the design could not have anticipated:

- **Hallucination** — top-decile insertions, mean WER 0.820, maximum insertion rate
  **6.15**, meaning one response for which Whisper emitted roughly seven times as many
  words as were spoken. It is by far the most dramatic ASR failure in the corpus and it has
  **no residual effect on scoring at all** (+0.017, p = .68).
- **Total ASR failure** — empty transcript. **Zero of 876.** The modelling pipeline carries
  an `n_words > 0` filter for this case and it never fires. A case class that turns out to
  be empty is a finding, since it is the failure mode a reader is most likely to assume.

The table needs one instruction to be read correctly, and it is in the report: **read the
signed-error column against the human-score column, not against zero.** Every
badly-transcribed class is also a low-proficiency class (mean 3.07–3.62 against the clean
class's 4.33), and band compression over-scores low-proficiency responses regardless of
their transcript. The class table is descriptive; the partial correlations are the test.
The clean class makes the point by being *under*-scored by 0.245 — near-perfect
transcripts, larger errors, in the other direction, because it is the strong-speaker class.

## Consistency: the design specified a figure that cannot exist here

The Evaluation design defines consistency as QWK between two independent LLM rubric-scoring
passes, exempting deterministic feature models because a repeat run cannot disagree with
itself. **The LLM arm is exempt on the same grounds**, which is what the design did not
anticipate. `LoRABandScorer.score_batch` takes one forward pass, reads eight logits at
fixed token ids and takes an expectation or an argmax. There is no sampling anywhere in the
scorer. A second pass is bit-identical, so pass-to-pass QWK is 1.0 by construction, for the
trained arm and the untrained one alike.

Reporting 1.0 would have satisfied the criterion and measured nothing. The substitute uses
what §4 already produced on the 28th: the same untrained model over the same rows under
**two prompts** — blank band letters, and the same letters anchored to CEFR names. One
scorer, scored twice, under a benign rewording of its instructions.

| | expected | argmax |
|---|---|---|
| pass-to-pass QWK | 0.512 | 0.244 |
| pass-to-pass r | 0.717 | 0.420 |
| exact agreement | 39.6% | 18.5% |
| vs human, best of the two passes | 0.144 | 0.072 |

This costs nothing — both caches already existed — and it answers a question the 28th's
write-up left open without noticing. Self-agreement is a **ceiling** on agreement with
humans: a scorer cannot match a rater more closely than it matches itself. The untrained
arm's ceiling is 0.512 and it scores 0.144, a quarter of the way up. So the zero-shot floor
is **not** low because the prompt was arbitrary — a scorer held back by prompt sensitivity
would be pressed against that ceiling instead of sitting well below it. The 28th's finding
that the floor belongs to the model rather than the label scheme now has a second,
independent line of evidence behind it.

The instability is real all the same, and large: renaming the bands moves 60% of the
expectation decode's assignments and 81% of the argmax decode's. It is validity that is
missing here, not reliability.

No equivalent figure exists for the fine-tuned arm. Getting one means a second fine-tune
under a reworded prompt — a training run, not a decode — so it is stated as absent.

## The criterion that was retired

"Labelled feedback examples", tagged useful / vague / unfair / overconfident, presupposes a
scorer that produces feedback. Nothing in this project ever did, and nothing was planned to
once the design settled on regression and ordinal decoding. It is retired in the amendment
rather than quietly dropped, because the value of a design document is precisely that it
records what was promised and not delivered. Feedback quality is a real evaluation
dimension and this project does not cover it.

The stretch goal — wav2vec2 embeddings plus a regression head, and SHAP attribution — is
declared out of scope on the reasoning that both would restate results already in hand.
wav2vec2 is the cheaper version of the question notebook 05 answered at greater cost, and
it came back negative there; SHAP on a 17-feature forest whose dominant feature is known to
be word count would confirm what the word-count baseline already states directly.

## The licence scan, promoted — and what it found

The pre-push licence check has been re-derived by hand every session and described in prose
in each session report, which makes it a habit rather than a control. It is now
`scripts/licence_scan.py`: three checks against the corpus itself — every utterance id,
every speaker id, and every five-word run of every cached transcript — over git-tracked
files by default, or `--history` for every blob reachable from any ref.

Writing it down found something the habit had missed for four sessions. **`src/speechlab/
data.py` carried a real utterance id and speaker id in a comment**, and notebook 01 carried
the same pair in a markdown cell and a code comment, in the shape:

```
# utteranceID '<real utterance id>' -> speakerID '<real speaker id>'
```

(redacted here, for the obvious reason — the scan flagged this very paragraph on the first
run after it was written, which is the check earning its place.) An illustrative example of
the join key, written while explaining how a speaker id is
recovered from an utterance id — and a real identifier of a real corpus participant,
committed to a public repository since the `src/` refactor in early June. Replaced with
`SIXXXX-00000-P30000`, which is verified against the corpus as not being anyone's id.

The reason the habit missed it is instructive: every session's manual check scanned
**notebook outputs**, on the reasoning that `utt` and `speaker` are used freely in cell
source. That reasoning conflates a column name with an id value. Column names are what
appears in source legitimately; a literal id has no innocent reason to be there, in a
notebook or in a module. The first version of the script inherited the same blind spot and
found only the `.py` occurrence; widening it to notebook *source* found the other two.
Markdown cells are rendered prose in any case, so excluding them was never defensible.

The scan also produced exactly the false positive its own docstring predicts: notebook 01's
editorial aside, "*This is the most important comparison in the notebook*", collides with
something a candidate said. Learner speech is ordinary English and 68,294 distinct 5-grams
is a lot of surface area. That is the scan working, not failing, so the script carries an
`ADJUDICATED` allowlist of cleared phrases **with the reason each was cleared** — and
deliberately no equivalent for ids, since an id hit has no innocent explanation and must
not be suppressible.

**The two blobs remain in git history.** Fixing forward removes them from the working tree
and from every future checkout, but `--history` still reports them at `f27f4ee3` and
`e3b93622`. Rewriting published history is a destructive operation on a pushed repository
and is not something to do unannounced; it is flagged here as an open decision rather than
taken.

## Findings

1. **Aggregate WER hid a real, small, opposite-signed effect.** Deletion rate keeps a
   partial correlation of −0.119 (p = .004) with signed error where WER keeps −0.014
   (p = .73), and it points at under-scoring rather than the over-scoring the raw
   correlation advertised. It replicates on a second, mechanically unrelated scorer.
2. **The most dramatic ASR failure is the one that does not matter.** Whisper looping to
   seven times the spoken length has no residual effect on the score; quiet deletion does.
3. **Self-agreement bounds agreement with humans, and the zero-shot arm is far under its
   own bound** — 0.144 against a 0.512 ceiling. That closes off "the prompt was arbitrary"
   as an explanation of the floor.
4. **The specified consistency metric was unmeasurable and would have looked measurable.**
   Pass-to-pass QWK is 1.0 by construction for every arm here. Reported without comment it
   would have read as a perfectly reliable scorer.
5. **Two deliverables were missing and no results-driven review would have found them.**
   §9's five recommendations are all generated from what the results showed. The design
   document is the only thing that remembers what was promised, and nothing had been
   reading it for three sessions.
6. **A real utterance and speaker id had been committed since June**, in a comment
   explaining the join key, and four sessions of manual licence checks did not see it
   because they scanned notebook *outputs* and not source. Writing the check down as
   `scripts/licence_scan.py` found it on first run. A control that exists only as a habit
   inherits the blind spots of whoever is exercising it.
