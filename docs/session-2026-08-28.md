# Session report — 28 August 2026: the baseline the LoRA arm never had

Yesterday's session shipped a fifth scoring arm and a negative result: a LoRA-fine-tuned
transcript scorer at **QWK 0.446** against the Random Forest's **0.558**, below the
word-count floor of 0.500. Today's session asked the one question that write-up could not
answer — *did the fine-tuning do anything?* — and found the answer was missing rather than
negative.

**The untrained floor is QWK 0.144. Fine-tuning is worth +0.302, roughly a tripling.** The
arm's problem is its ceiling, not its training.

## The gap

Reading back the finished report, the comparison table has five arms and no baseline
belonging to the fifth. Every number 0.446 was set against — 0.558, 0.548, 0.500 — comes
from a *different pipeline*. Nothing in the write-up establishes that the 400 iterations of
LoRA changed anything at all, and the phrase the result invites ("worse after fine-tuning")
turns out to be comparing the arm to its neighbours rather than to itself.

A grep confirmed no zero-shot run existed anywhere in the repo. This was not a difficult
experiment that got deferred; it was a cheap one that was never thought of. No training, no
cross-validation — nothing is fitted, so every row is held out by construction — just one
forward pass over 876 prompts. It cost about four minutes of compute against the hours the
trained arm took.

## What it needed

Almost nothing, which is the uncomfortable part. `LoRABandScorer.__init__` already took
`adapter_path: str | None = None` and passed it straight to `mlx_lm.load`, so base weights
were a supported configuration from the day the class was written. The band distribution is
read off the logits at the `A`–`H` token ids rather than free-generated, so the scheme also
survives an untrained model that would rather answer in prose.

Rather than write a separate driver, `--zero-shot` went onto `scripts/finetune_lora.py`. The
motive is fold parity: a standalone script would have had to copy the fold assignment out of
the trained arm's cache, whereas the flag runs the same loop over the same `GroupKFold` call
on the same dataframe, so rows and folds match **structurally** rather than by assertion.
The notebook still asserts it — row count, fold index and `y_true` all checked against the
trained arm — but the assertion is now a smoke alarm rather than the load-bearing wall.

## The confound, and the second run

A low zero-shot score has two explanations, and only one of them is about the model.
`build_prompt` deliberately labels the bands `A`–`H` with no CEFR names attached, and its
docstring says why: blank letters "arrive blank and only have to learn the ordering",
whereas `B2` carries pretrained baggage the fine-tune would have to fight. That is the right
trade for training and a handicap at zero-shot — the untrained model is being asked to use a
scale nobody gave it the key to. A floor measured that way cannot distinguish *cannot judge
proficiency* from *cannot guess what H means*.

So a second variant, `--rubric`, names the anchors from `data_gates.md` gate 1
(1=A1 … 5=C1). Half-steps are left unnamed on purpose: the corpus scores at 0.5 intervals
but CEFR has no name for the midpoint, and inventing `B1+` would put a label in the prompt
that no rater ever used.

Two guards went in with it. The plain prompt is **byte-identical** to the one the trained
arm used — verified against a literal, not eyeballed — because otherwise the comparison
silently stops being like-for-like. And `--rubric` without `--zero-shot` is a hard error:
`write_fold_data` builds training rows through `build_record`, which does not take the flag,
so training with it would fit on plain prompts and score on anchored ones.
`assert_prompt_parity` cannot catch that — it checks the built string against itself — so
the combination is refused rather than allowed to cost QWK quietly.

## Result

| arm | r | MAE | QWK | bands used (argmax) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| zero-shot, blank labels (expected) | 0.228 | 0.886 | 0.144 | — |
| zero-shot, blank labels (argmax) | 0.151 | 1.525 | 0.072 | 2 of 8 |
| zero-shot, CEFR-anchored (expected) | 0.281 | 0.728 | 0.122 | — |
| zero-shot, CEFR-anchored (argmax) | 0.126 | 1.137 | 0.042 | 5 of 8 |
| **fine-tuned (argmax)** | 0.505 | 0.511 | **0.446** | 5 of 8 |
| fine-tuned (expected) | 0.550 | 0.523 | 0.415 | — |

Untrained, the model is barely reading the transcript. With blank labels it puts **797 of
876 responses in the top band** and the other 79 in the bottom one — a two-class answer with
90% of its probability mass on the two endpoints of an eight-band scale.

Anchoring fixes the collapse without fixing the ranking. Five bands get used, MAE improves
0.886 → 0.728, r improves 0.228 → 0.281 — and QWK does not rise; it slips to 0.122. The
model relocates its guess from "everyone is H" to "everyone is G" and orders candidates no
better for it. That is the answer to the confound: the floor is the model's, not the label
scheme's.

Every pre-existing figure in notebook 05 reproduced exactly on re-execution — RF 0.558, the
WER mediation table, the gold ablation — so the new section is an addition, not a
disturbance.

## Two measurement traps this surfaced

Both are cases where a summary statistic reports health that isn't there, and both were
live in the existing write-up.

**Prediction range is worthless when a decode collapses.** The blank-label argmax spans the
full 3.5 of the human range while using two of eight bands. Notebook 05 §3 leaned on
`pred_range` as its warning sign for compression, and against this arm it would have raised
none. The band marginal is what actually carries the failure, and it is now printed beside
the metrics — with a `bands used` row, since that is the number a reader would otherwise
have to reconstruct by eye.

**Spread is not calibration.** The untrained expectation decode has predicted sd 0.594
against the fine-tuned arm's 0.391 and a human 0.733. On spread alone it is the
better-calibrated scorer of the two, and it ranks three times worse. Being distributed over
roughly the right interval for entirely the wrong reasons looks like good calibration in
every statistic except the ones that matter.

## Findings

1. **Fine-tuning tripled QWK.** 0.144 → 0.446, +0.302. Yesterday's negative result stands
   as a statement about where the trained arm lands, and is now explicitly *not* a statement
   about the training having failed.
2. **The base model cannot do this task**, and not because of the label scheme — anchoring
   the CEFR names moves the guess without improving the ranking.
3. **The floor was cheap and was simply not thought of.** Four minutes of compute against
   hours for the arm it grounds, using a code path that already existed.
4. **Two summary statistics in the existing write-up were reporting health that wasn't
   there** — prediction range under class collapse, predicted spread as a calibration proxy.

## What this changes elsewhere

Recommendation 1 of `reports/evaluation_report.md` previously said only that a model change
must beat the word-count baseline. It now also requires an arm to report **its own untrained
or ablated floor**, because §4 shipped that question unanswered and the omission was not
visible from the table.

The "test at larger scale" follow-up gains a little support it did not have: +0.302 from 400
iterations at rank 8 is a steep part of a curve, which is weak evidence the curve has not
flattened. Weak, and worth stating as weak — it is an argument from the shape of one gain,
not from any measurement above 1.5B.

## Next

Unchanged from yesterday, and none of it is displaced by this session: the clean
transcript-quality control (`--restrict-to`, the de-confounding run), `save_every` finer
than `iters` for a best-checkpoint fallback, decode calibration fitted on an inner split,
and scale as the untested lever.

One addition, small: the zero-shot floor is two prompts, not a prompt search. Neither used
few-shot examples, chain-of-thought or a system message, and a 1.5B model is prompt-
sensitive. The claim is that the floor is low under the arm's own prompt and under a fair
anchored version of it — not that no prompt could do better.
