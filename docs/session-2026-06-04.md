# Session report — 4 June 2026: project inception to first published results

## What I built

In one session I took the CEFR Spoken Response Scoring Lab from an idea to a public repository with real evaluation results: a scoped design document, a verified dataset, a working ASR→features→model→evaluation pipeline, and a first weighted-kappa score against human ratings.

## Design before code

I ran the idea through a structured design review rather than starting with code. The session forced three decisions I consider the most consequential of the project:

- **Real data over self-recorded audio.** I replaced my original plan to record 30–100 responses myself with the Speak & Improve Corpus 2025 (Cambridge/SANDI challenge) — real L2 learner speech scored by trained annotators. This upgraded every downstream deliverable from pseudo-labels to genuine measurement.
- **Licence governance from commit zero.** The corpus is research-licensed, so `data/` is gitignored from the first commit, notebooks display aggregates only, and I verified zero data paths in git history before publishing. I treated this as a responsible-AI requirement, not housekeeping.
- **Verification gates before headline metrics.** I refused to commit to kappa or fairness deliverables until I had confirmed the score scale, the label structure, and gold-transcript availability from the corpus metadata itself.

## Data verification findings

The gates paid for themselves immediately (full detail in `reports/data_gates.md`):

- Scores are 0–6 (≈CEFR) at 0.5 intervals, per part, from Linguaskill criteria — finer than I had assumed.
- Single consensus labels only — so I renamed the "inter-rater reliability" deliverable to model–human agreement plus intra-scorer consistency, rather than claiming an analysis the data cannot support.
- Gold transcripts ship in three forms (disfluent, fluent, grammar-corrected) plus word-level disfluency marks — richer than hoped.
- **No L1 metadata exists**, so I pivoted the fairness analysis from L1 slices to audio-quality and proficiency-band slices, which the corpus does support.

## The vertical slice and first results

I built the entire pipeline end-to-end on 100 Part-3 responses before building any of it properly: faster-whisper transcription with word timestamps, eleven hand-built fluency features (speech rate, articulation rate, pause statistics, length of run, lexical diversity), and Ridge regression evaluated with 5-fold `cross_val_predict` to avoid wasting scarce labels on a noisy test split.

| Model | Pearson r | MAE | QWK |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean predictor | — | 0.558 | 0.000 |
| Word-count Ridge | 0.648 | 0.418 | 0.580 |
| Full Ridge (11 features) | 0.671 | 0.433 | 0.621 |

Three findings I consider portfolio-grade:

1. **Word count alone achieves QWK 0.58** — ten further features buy only +0.04. Quantity of speech carries most of the proficiency signal, a known result in fluency research that I have now reproduced rather than read about.
2. **Whisper erased 100% of filled pauses** — the feature died at exactly zero across all 100 responses. A clean, quantified demonstration of ASR errors propagating into feature space, and exhibit A for the error-analysis report.
3. **Type-token ratio correlated negatively with proficiency** — the textbook length-sensitivity artefact, which justifies replacing it with MTLD in the next notebook.

## Tools and decisions

uv-managed Python 3.12 environment; faster-whisper (CTranslate2) chosen over openai-whisper for ~8–10× CPU speed without needing PyTorch yet; scikit-learn pipelines with the scaler inside the fold to avoid leakage; quadratic weighted kappa on the 0.5-step ordinal grid. Published to GitHub under my personal account with project-local git identity, history rewritten pre-push to fix attribution.
